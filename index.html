1945 — 1973

The Promise

For thirty years, America built a specific kind of future for its working people. A house. A single income that covered it. Healthcare that came with the job. A degree the next generation could afford.

These are the voices of Americans who lived inside that promise — before the wages stopped tracking productivity, before the contract changed.

The Architecture
The Architecture

What was built, deliberately

The GI Bill sent eight million veterans to college on the federal government's account. The FHA drove homeownership from 44% in 1940 to 62% by 1960. Union membership reached 35% of the workforce. For the people inside this architecture, it worked.

"In 1960, a median household spent 44 cents of every earned dollar on the full basket of middle-class life. That 44 cents would become $1.20 by 2024."

But the architecture had exclusions built in. The FHA's own manuals recommended against insuring mortgages in racially mixed neighborhoods. Black veterans found the GI Bill administered locally by the same people who enforced segregation. The Dream was always rationed.

What is the Dream Index?

A measurement, not a metaphor

The Dream Index measures the annual cost of a canonical middle-class American life — a median home mortgage, family health insurance, one year of public college tuition, center-based childcare, vehicle ownership and operation, and a family's food budget — expressed as a fraction of median household income.

When the index reads 0.44, as it did in 1960, the basket consumed 44 cents of every dollar the median household earned. When it reads 1.19, as it does in 2024, the basket costs 19% more than the median household earns in an entire year. All values are expressed in real 2024 dollars, adjusted for inflation using the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U).

The index does not measure subjective wellbeing or lifestyle preferences. It measures a single arithmetic fact: can the median American household afford what was once considered a standard American life?

1973 — 1984

The Break

In 1973, wages stopped tracking productivity. On September 19, 1977 — Black Monday — Youngstown Sheet and Tube closed, laying off 5,000 workers.

It was not an anomaly. It was the beginning of a pattern that would hollow out manufacturing America over the next fifty years.

"Aftermath of the 1977 Youngstown Steel Mill Closure" — YouTube

The Mechanism

When labor stopped being rewarded

Union membership began its long decline from 35% toward 10%. Trade policy opened manufacturing to global competition. Corporate governance shifted toward shareholder returns. Each change redirected productivity gains away from workers and toward capital.

"If wages had tracked productivity since 1973, the median American worker would earn approximately $20 more per hour today."

The figure below makes this concrete. Nonfarm business output per hour — a standard measure of economic productivity — is indexed alongside real manufacturing wages, both set to 100 in 1960. Until 1973 the lines move together, confirming that workers were capturing the gains of the economy they built. After 1973, they do not. The gap between what the economy produced and what workers were paid has widened every decade since. The shaded area beneath the divergence is not abstract: it represents wages that were earned by the economy but not distributed to the workers who generated them. The figure also shows a third line — what wages would be today if they had tracked productivity since 1973. The distance between that hypothetical line and actual wages is the arithmetic of the asset contract.

Coal Country — McDowell County, WV

Extraction economy abandoned after the asset ran out

McDowell County peaked in the 1950s with 100,000 people and wages above the national median. When the coal ran out and strip mining mechanized extraction, nothing replaced it. Today: 18,000 residents. Median income 47% of national.

The productivity gains went to the top 1%

If wages didn't capture the productivity gains since 1973, who did? The Federal Reserve's Distributional Financial Accounts answer the question for 1989 onward: the top 1% expanded its share of US household wealth from 22.9% to 31.2%. The bottom 50% shrank from 3.5% to 2.5%.

What Worked, and For Whom

The contract did not fail everyone equally

The asset contract created genuine winners alongside genuine losers. Two groups illustrate what success looked like — and what enabled it.

Women's entry into the professional workforce is one of the most consequential economic transformations of the 20th century. Women's labor force participation rose from 38% in 1960 to 58% today. Women now earn the majority of college degrees. The gender wage gap has narrowed substantially. These are real gains — not statistical artifacts, not incomplete progress dressed as completion, but measurable improvements in access, opportunity, and economic independence.

Immigrants who arrived in the United States before 2000 and purchased homes have, in many cases, accumulated wealth comparable to or exceeding native-born Americans of similar education. The first-generation story of arrival, labor, and eventual stability is real and documented. In communities across America, it happened.

"The asset contract is not broken for everyone. It is broken for those who arrived after the prices changed."

Both stories share a structural feature: timing. Women who entered professional careers in the 1970s and 1980s, when credential returns were rising and housing was still affordable, built wealth. Immigrants who arrived before 2000 and bought homes at pre-crossing prices built wealth. The mechanism that rewarded them is the same mechanism that now forecloses the same path to those who come after.

This is not an argument against women's advancement or immigrant success. It is an argument about structure. The asset contract produced winners. The question this project asks is whether those wins are reproducible — and what the data says is that they are not, at current entry prices, without fundamental structural change.

Six Decades of Economic Divergence

What national averages conceal

The national Dream Index describes an average — but averages conceal as much as they reveal. To understand how the asset contract operates in practice, this project examines five communities whose economic trajectories have diverged sharply from national benchmarks since 1960. Each place was chosen because it embodies a distinct structural mechanism: racial exclusion from the original postwar architecture, extraction economy collapse, manufacturing displacement, colonial land dispossession, and concentrated asset-economy gains.

The three metrics — median income relative to national, poverty rate, and homeownership rate — are measured at each decennial census from 1960 to 2020 using county-level data from the National Historical Geographic Information System (NHGIS). What you are looking at is not natural variation. It is the geography of policy decisions made between 1944 and 2000 whose consequences are still compounding.

Income vs. Wealth

The gap that income policy cannot close

The racial income gap has narrowed by six percentage points in 57 years. That is real progress. But the racial wealth gap — the difference in what households own, not what they earn — has barely moved. Black households held 9.3 cents of wealth for every dollar held by white households in 1989. Today they hold 12 cents.

This is the asset contract's central evidence. Income and wages move together when the economy grows. Wealth compounds independently of current income — it accumulates from assets bought decades ago, from inheritances, from equity in homes purchased before prices ran away. A policy that addresses only wages addresses only half the mechanism. The other half is the asset itself: who owns it, who was prevented from owning it, and how many decades of compounding that prevention represents.

These five communities did not experience what came next as a national statistic. Each had already lived its own version of the Break. But in 2011, the national mathematics crossed a threshold that made their local conditions the new median American condition. The Dream Index exceeded 1.0 for the first time. The basket cost more than the median household earned. What had been exceptional became average.

2000 — 2015

The Crossing

In 2011, for the first time in American history, the cost of a canonical middle-class life exceeded the median household income.

The Dream Index crossed 1.0. The math that had been tilting for thirty years finally broke through.

"Inside America's Capital of Despair: Youngstown" — YouTube

The Inflection

When the math finally broke

The financial crisis of 2008 dropped median incomes while healthcare and education costs kept rising. By 2011, the accumulated weight of thirty years of divergence reached the tipping point.

Low interest rates between 2012 and 2020 created a temporary window — falling mortgage payments made housing affordable despite rising prices. Americans who bought in that window got the last affordable entry point. In 2022, rates doubled. The window closed permanently.

"The rate floor was a reprieve, not a recovery. The Dream Index has not returned below 1.0."

The Consequences

Economic statistics are human statistics

Median age at first marriage rose by six years since 1960. Americans say in surveys they want more children than they have — they cite cost as the reason. The U.S. is the only wealthy country where life expectancy declined after 2014.

"The country is paying its bills in lifespans and childlessness."

Working-age Americans without college degrees started dying earlier in the 2000s from suicide, overdose, and alcohol. These deaths map almost precisely onto the geography of economic abandonment shown in the Five Americas data.

The Price Paid in Years and Choices

Economic distress does not stay in financial statements

When households cannot afford the basket of basic life, they respond rationally — by restructuring the shape of their lives. They delay marriage because forming a household requires two incomes and a deposit they cannot save. They have fewer children than they say they want because childcare costs more than rent in most American cities. They move back in with parents because renting alone consumes more than 30% of take-home income. And in communities where economic abandonment is most complete, they die younger.

The four indicators below — median age at first marriage, total fertility rate, share of adults in multigenerational households, and life expectancy at birth — are not separate phenomena. They are four different measurements of the same underlying pressure. Each has moved in the direction the Dream Index would predict, and each movement accelerated after 2000, when the index began its final climb toward 1.0.

This is what the data looks like when you measure it not in dollars, but in years of life and choices foreclosed.

Recommendations

What the evidence demands

Federal policymakers should fund universal asset-building accounts at birth because our analysis found the racial wealth gap has barely closed in six decades despite narrowing income gaps — wealth cannot be addressed through wage policy alone.

State legislators should preempt restrictive zoning because our analysis found housing costs have outpaced wages in every metro area since 1990, with supply constraint as the primary structural driver.

Congress should restore the GI Bill as an asset-transfer program because our analysis found contemporary veterans' wealth outcomes diverge from WWII-era veterans specifically where the original GI Bill provided asset access.

"The architecture of the Dream was built through specific decisions. Rebuilding it requires specific decisions — not generic growth, but targeted structural repair."
What This Project Found

Seven findings. One story.

The evidence assembled across this project points to a single structural shift: the American economy transitioned, between roughly 1973 and 2011, from a system that delivered middle-class life through wages to one that requires asset ownership, credentialed employment, or inherited capital. The seven findings below are not separate arguments. They are seven different measurements of the same underlying change, each confirming it from a distinct angle — the basket, the labor market, the racial wealth gap, the geography of abandonment, and the shape of American lives.

Methodology & Data

The Dream Index measures the cost of housing, healthcare, education, childcare, transportation, and food as a multiple of median household income. All values in real 2024 dollars using CPI-U as the deflator. Primary healthcare measure uses the full KFF family premium (crosses 1.0 in 2011). Conservative lower bound uses household exposure only (crosses 2022).

Data sources: FRED (38 series), KFF Employer Health Benefits Survey (1999–2024), NCES Digest Table 330.10 (1963+), NHGIS county-level census data (1960–2020), CDC NCHS life expectancy and birth rates, BLS Consumer Price Indices.

Five places: Counties aggregated to place level using population-weighted averages. Pine Ridge merges FIPS 46-102 and 46-113 as one continuous series. All income comparisons use nominal values divided by national nominal median — the ratio cancels inflation, no deflation required.
