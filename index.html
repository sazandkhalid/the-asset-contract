The Architecture
The Architecture

What was built, deliberately

Before the numbers, the policies. This chapter is a walk through the legislative architecture that built the postwar American middle class, and the laws that later disassembled it. The Dream Index, the measurement that tracks what those policies produced, arrives in the next chapter.

The GI Bill sent eight million veterans to college on the federal government's account. The FHA drove homeownership from 44% in 1940 to 62% by 1960. Union membership reached 35% of the workforce. For the people inside this architecture, it worked.

But the architecture had exclusions built in. The FHA's own manuals recommended against insuring mortgages in racially mixed neighborhoods. Black veterans found the GI Bill administered locally by the same people who enforced segregation. The Dream was always rationed.

"In 1960, a median household spent 44 cents of every earned dollar on the full basket of middle-class life. That 44 cents would become $1.20 by 2024."

1945, 1973

The Promise

For thirty years, America built a specific kind of future for its working people. A house. A single income that covered it. Healthcare that came with the job. A degree the next generation could afford.

These are the voices of Americans who lived inside that promise, before the wages stopped tracking productivity, before the contract changed.

Studs Terkel, "Working Americans on Home Ownership" , 1971. Clip: 1:28–1:55.

What is the Dream Index?

How do you put a number on a broken promise?

The Dream Index measures the annual cost of a canonical middle-class American life, a median home mortgage, family health insurance, one year of public college tuition, center-based childcare, vehicle ownership and operation, and a family's food budget, expressed as a fraction of median household income.

When the index reads 0.44, as it did in 1960, the basket consumed 44 cents of every dollar the median household earned. When it reads 1.19, as it does in 2024, the basket costs 19% more than the median household earns in an entire year. All values are expressed in real 2024 dollars, adjusted for inflation using the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U).

The index does not measure subjective wellbeing or lifestyle preferences. It measures a single arithmetic fact: can the median American household afford what was once considered a standard American life?

The American Dream Became Unaffordable in 2011

01 · The frame

Two trajectories, one country

For sixty-five years, two numbers measured the same idea: what an American household earned, and what an American life cost.

The first is median household income, adjusted for inflation. The second is the Dream Basket a canonical middle-class life: housing, healthcare, education, childcare, transportation, and food.

For half a century, these two lines were inseparable. Then they weren't.

02 · What households earned

Income rose, but slowly

In real 2024 dollars, the typical American household earned $41,348 in 1960. By 2024, it earned $83,753.

A doubling, but a doubling spread across sixty-four years. Roughly 1.1% real growth per year, on average. Less in the most recent three decades.

03 · What a life cost

The basket rose faster

The Dream Basket cost $18,213 in 1960. By 2024 it cost $100,083, a 5.5x increase in real terms.

Housing, healthcare, education, and childcare all rose faster than wages. Consumer goods got cheaper. The basket was reshaped by what was possible to import (clothing, electronics) and what was not (housing, the human time of teachers, doctors, and child caregivers).

04 · Four chapters

The story has four periods

Postwar Promise (1960–1973): the basket cost less than half of household income.

Erosion (1973–2007): wages decoupled from productivity. The gap closed.

Crisis (2008–2011): the financial collapse accelerated what was already happening.

Rate floor and after (2012–today): cheap mortgages briefly disguised the problem. When rates rose in 2022, the gap reopened.

05 · The crossing

In 2011, the lines crossed

For the first time in measurable American history, the cost of a canonical middle-class life exceeded what the typical household earned.

Basket: $70,372. Income: $69,804. Gap: $568.

$568 is approximately three months of childcare for one child, or 60% of community college tuition for a year. A small number that crossed a meaningful line.

06 · The gap widens

And then it kept widening

The rust-colored area is the gap. Every year the basket cost more than the median household earned, by an amount visible on the chart.

By 2024, the gap was $16,330, equivalent to one full year of in-state college tuition, or six months of family health insurance premiums. Or roughly four months of rent for a median two-bedroom apartment in any American metro.

08 · The argument

This is not a personal failure

The crossing happened to everyone at once. It is not a story of individual choices, individual failures, or individual generations.

The basket and the income are calculated identically for every American household. The crossing is the structural fact this project exists to document.

The rest of this site explains how it happened which mechanisms broke first, who was protected, who was not, and what it costs to live in a country where the arithmetic of a middle-class life no longer works.

Consumer goods collapsed in real price. The essentials for building a life exploded. America made consumption affordable and existence expensive.

1973, 1984

The Break

In 1973, wages stopped tracking productivity. On September 19, 1977, Black Monday, Youngstown Sheet and Tube closed, laying off 5,000 workers.

It was not an anomaly. It was the beginning of a pattern that would hollow out manufacturing America over the next fifty years.

The Mechanism
The Mechanism

When labor stopped being rewarded

Union membership began its long decline from 35% toward 10%. Trade policy opened manufacturing to global competition. Corporate governance shifted toward shareholder returns. Each change redirected productivity gains away from workers and toward capital.

The figure below makes this concrete. Nonfarm business output per hour, a standard measure of economic productivity, is indexed alongside real manufacturing wages, both set to 100 in 1960. Until 1973 the lines move together, confirming that workers were capturing the gains of the economy they built. After 1973, they do not. The gap between what the economy produced and what workers were paid has widened every decade since.

The shaded area beneath the divergence is not abstract: it represents wages that were earned by the economy but not distributed to the workers who generated them. The figure also shows a third line, what wages would be today if they had tracked productivity since 1973. The distance between that hypothetical line and actual wages is the arithmetic of the asset contract.

"If wages had tracked productivity since 1973, the median American worker would earn approximately $20 more per hour today."
Figure 5, Wages vs. Productivity

Wages vs. Productivity, 1960–2024

Both indexed to 100 in 1960. The lines track together through the postwar period, then separate in 1973. The gap is the engine of the asset contract.

Figure 6, Hours of work

What the basket costs in weeks of full-time work.

An alternative way to read the divergence: instead of inflation-adjusted dollars, count weeks of median-wage work to cover one year of each component, in 1960 vs. 2024. Healthcare went from about a week of work to roughly four months. The total basket tripled.

Figure 7, Where the gains went

The productivity gains went to the top 1%

If wages didn't capture the productivity gains since 1973, who did? The Federal Reserve's Distributional Financial Accounts answer the question for 1989 onward: the top 1% expanded its share of US household wealth from 22.9% to 31.2%. The bottom 50% shrank from 3.5% to 2.5%.

What Worked, and For Whom

The contract did not fail everyone equally

The asset contract created genuine winners alongside genuine losers. Two groups illustrate what success looked like, and what enabled it.

Women's entry into the professional workforce is one of the most consequential economic transformations of the 20th century. Women's labor force participation rose from 38% in 1960 to 58% today. Women now earn the majority of college degrees. The gender wage gap has narrowed substantially. These are real gains, not statistical artifacts, not incomplete progress dressed as completion, but measurable improvements in access, opportunity, and economic independence.

Immigrants who arrived in the United States before 2000 and purchased homes have, in many cases, accumulated wealth comparable to or exceeding native-born Americans of similar education. The first-generation story of arrival, labor, and eventual stability is real and documented. In communities across America, it happened.

"The asset contract is not broken for everyone. It is broken for those who arrived after the prices changed."

Both stories share a structural feature: timing. Women who entered professional careers in the 1970s and 1980s, when credential returns were rising and housing was still affordable, built wealth. Immigrants who arrived before 2000 and bought homes at pre-crossing prices built wealth. The mechanism that rewarded them is the same mechanism that now forecloses the same path to those who come after.

This is not an argument against women's advancement or immigrant success. It is an argument about structure. The asset contract produced winners. The question this project asks is whether those wins are reproducible, and what the data says is that they are not, at current entry prices, without fundamental structural change.

The right panel shows this shift alongside the Dream Index. Both lines rise. Their relationship is not a coincidence or a contradiction. It is the asset contract adapting.

Five Americas, Five Mechanisms

Why these five communities

The national Dream Index is a single number. The country is not. To average a contract that worked for some and never reached others is to describe nothing accurately.

This chapter looks at five specific American communities. They are not random examples. Together they form a typology of asset contract failure, each one representing a structurally distinct way the postwar economic deal either never extended, was withdrawn, was made obsolete, or worked as designed and still broke.

The Mississippi Delta shows the contract deliberately denied. Pine Ridge shows it never extended. Coal Country shows it extended and then abandoned when the underlying asset ran out. The Rust Belt shows it extended and then made obsolete by globalization. The Bay Area and NYC show what happens when the contract works exactly as designed, and locally breaks anyway.

Five communities. Five mechanisms. One contract, five different ways of failing.

The Asset Gap

How the gap is made.

Act I, The income gap

Black households earn 64¢ for every $1 white households earn.

64¢

This gap has narrowed. In 1967 the ratio was 55¢. Six decades later it sits at 64¢, a 9-cent gain across two generations.

Income narrows when wages rise. Wages have risen, slowly, unevenly, but they have risen. The labor market, for all its faults, partially responded.

Act II, The wealth gap

But Black households own 12¢ for every $1 of wealth white households own.

12¢

In 1989 the ratio was 9¢. Today it is 12¢. Three cents in 33 years. The wealth gap has barely moved while the income gap has slowly narrowed.

This is the question the chapter exists to answer: why?

Act III, The mechanism

Imagine two households in 1989. Identical in every way except one thing.

Both earn the same income. Both save 8% of it. Both invest in assets that return 6% a year.

The only difference is their starting balance. The median white household held $100,000 in assets. The median Black household held $9,300.

This is not because of effort. It is because of sixty prior years of denied access to the asset contract, the FHA mortgages, the GI Bill, the union pensions, the suburbs.

Act IV, Compounding

Project them forward 35 years.

Same savings rate. Same return. Same effort. The trajectories diverge, not because one earned more, but because compound interest pays a return on what you already own.

The absolute gap widens with every year. Compounding does not close gaps. It amplifies them.

Act V, Today

This is the wealth gap today.

The median white household has roughly $285,000 in net worth. The median Black household has roughly $44,000.

The gap is not made by who earned more this year. It is made by who was allowed to buy assets sixty years ago, and who has therefore been compounding ever since.

This is the asset contract's central trick. It rewards what you already own.

The Consequences

Economic distress does not stay in financial statements.

When households cannot afford the basket of basic life, they restructure the shape of their lives. They delay marriage because forming a household requires two incomes and a deposit they cannot save. They have fewer children than they say they want because childcare costs more than rent in most American cities. They move back in with parents because renting alone consumes more than 30% of take-home income. And in communities where economic abandonment is most complete, they die younger.

The four indicators below, median age at first marriage, total fertility rate, share of adults in multigenerational households, life expectancy at birth, are not separate phenomena. They are four measurements of the same underlying pressure. Each has moved in the direction the Dream Index would predict. Each accelerated after 2000, when the index began its final climb toward 1.0.

This is what the data looks like when you measure it not in dollars, but in years of life and choices foreclosed.

The Reckoning

Seven findings. One contract to rewrite.

The evidence assembled across this project points to a single structural shift: the American economy transitioned, between roughly 1973 and 2011, from a system that delivered middle-class life through wages to one that requires asset ownership, credentialed employment, or inherited capital.

The seven memos that follow are not separate arguments. They are seven different measurements of the same underlying change, and seven concrete interventions, each tied to one mechanism documented in the preceding chapters. Each could be delivered to a working policymaker today.

The architecture of the Dream was built through specific decisions. Rebuilding it requires specific decisions, not generic growth, but targeted structural repair.

Methodology & Data

The Dream Index measures the cost of housing, healthcare, education, childcare, transportation, and food as a multiple of median household income. All values in real 2024 dollars using CPI-U as the deflator. Primary healthcare measure uses the full KFF family premium (crosses 1.0 in 2011). Conservative lower bound uses household exposure only (crosses 2022).

Data sources: FRED (38 series), KFF Employer Health Benefits Survey (1999–2024), NCES Digest Table 330.10 (1963+), NHGIS county-level census data (1960–2020), CDC NCHS life expectancy and birth rates, BLS Consumer Price Indices.

Five places: Counties aggregated to place level using population-weighted averages. Pine Ridge merges FIPS 46-102 and 46-113 as one continuous series. All income comparisons use nominal values divided by national nominal median, the ratio cancels inflation, no deflation required.

Full methodology, sensitivity analyses, and data files: dream_index_metadata.json
